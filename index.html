I — Overview

What Was
the Revolution?

An insurrection carried out by 13 of Great Britain's North American colonies, who won political independence and formed the United States of America — the culmination of a decade of growing tensions between the colonies and the British Crown.

Initially a civil war within the British Empire, the conflict evolved into an international war when France joined in 1778, followed by Spain in 1779. Naval power proved decisive, enabling the French to assist in the British surrender at Yorktown in 1781.

Salutary Neglect
For 150+ years Britain loosely enforced trade laws allowing colonies near self-governance. After the Seven Years War, Britain abruptly tightened control, shocking colonists accustomed to independence.
First Continental Congress (1774)
Asserted Britain could not subdue Massachusetts without interference from all colonies. Demanded repeal of the Coercive Acts — setting the stage for armed conflict the following year.

II — Timeline

Key Events of the Revolution

1765 – 1773
The Taxation Acts
Stamp Act, Townshend Acts, Tea Act — all imposed without colonial representation. "No taxation without representation" became the rallying cry of the Patriot movement.
March 5, 1770
Boston Massacre
British soldiers killed 5 colonists on King Street. Paul Revere's famous engraving spread outrage across all colonies.
Paul Revere's Engraving · 1770
December 1773
Boston Tea Party
Colonists dumped an entire British tea shipment into Boston Harbor. Britain responded with the Coercive Acts, deepening colonial resistance.
April 19, 1775
Lexington & Concord
The first shots of the Revolutionary War. The shot heard round the world — eight years of conflict had begun.
July 4, 1776
Declaration of Independence
Formal separation of the 13 colonies from Great Britain, drafted primarily by Thomas Jefferson.
October 1777
Battle of Saratoga
Decisive American victory. Burgoyne's entire army forced to surrender — convincing France to formally ally with the colonies.
1778 – 1779
France & Spain Enter
France joins via Treaty of Alliance. Spain follows as French ally. The colonial civil war becomes a global conflict.
October 19, 1781
Yorktown — The Surrender
Cornwallis surrenders to Washington. French naval power at the Chesapeake proved decisive. The war effectively ends.

September 3, 1783
Treaty of Paris
Official peace treaty signed. Great Britain formally recognizes the independence of the United States of America.

III — Key Figures

The People Who Shaped It

George Washington
Commander-in-Chief of the Continental Army. Won key victories at Boston, Trenton, Princeton & Yorktown. First president of the United States.
Thomas Jefferson
Primary author of the Declaration of Independence. Governor of Virginia, later 3rd president of the United States.
Benjamin Franklin
Diplomat in France. Lobbied for French support and helped secure the Treaty of Alliance. Senior member of the committee drafting the Declaration.
John Adams
Opposed the Stamp Act. Helped draft the Declaration. Negotiated the Treaty of Paris. Later the 2nd president of the United States.
Thomas Paine
Author of Common Sense (1776), an unprecedented bestseller that convinced countless colonists to embrace independence from Britain.
Marquis de Lafayette
French aristocrat who arrived in America at 19. Became one of Washington's most trusted generals and a key rallier of French military support.
Benedict Arnold
Skilled Continental commander at Ticonderoga, Quebec & Saratoga. Wounded twice, later defected to the British Army in 1780.
Nathanael Greene
Shifted from pacifist to commander. Criticized Congress for relying on short-term militiamen who lacked the fortitude for sustained warfare.
Horatio Gates
Forced Burgoyne to surrender at Saratoga (1777). Later disgraced at the Battle of Camden (1780).
Henry Knox
Transported 55 cannons 300 miles from Fort Ticonderoga to Cambridge. Commanded Continental Army artillery throughout the war.
Daniel Morgan
Frontiersman commander. Won the decisive victory over Banastre Tarleton at the Battle of Cowpens.
John Sullivan
General in multiple major campaigns, including a devastating campaign against Seneca & Cayuga nations on Washington's direct orders in 1779.
Rochambeau
Commander of French Army forces. Marched to Virginia with Washington. His expertise in siege warfare proved essential at Yorktown.
Friedrich von Steuben
Prussian "Drillmaster of the Revolution." Transformed the Continental Army at Valley Forge. Taught marching, bayonet use, and musket firing.
John Glover
Evacuated Washington's army across the East River after Long Island. Rowed troops across the icy Delaware on Christmas Night, 1776.
John Paul Jones
Scottish-born naval commander. Defeated HMS Serapis at Flamborough Head. Often called the "Father of the American Navy."
Bernardo de Galvez
Spanish Governor of Louisiana. Seized Baton Rouge, Natchez, Mobile, and Pensacola. Honorary U.S. citizen. Galveston, Texas named for him.
George III
King of Britain for 59 years. Backed taxation policies, hired Hessian mercenaries, declared the colonies in a state of rebellion in 1775.
Henry Clinton
Commander-in-Chief from 1778. Failed to reinforce Cornwallis at Yorktown. Bore much of the blame for Britain's defeat.
Charles Cornwallis
Led the British Southern campaign. Won at Camden. Surrendered at Yorktown on October 19, 1781, effectively ending the war.
William Howe
Commander-in-Chief 1775-78. Won Bunker Hill, Long Island, and Philadelphia. His march on Philadelphia contributed to the disaster at Saratoga.
John Burgoyne
"Gentleman Johnny." Led 1777 invasion from Canada. Abandoned without reinforcements, surrendered at Saratoga, triggering the French alliance.
Richard Howe
Admiral, brother of William. Commanded Royal Navy North America. Attempted peace negotiations before fighting began. Resigned 1778.
Lord Dunmore
Last royal Governor of Virginia. His 1775 proclamation offering freedom to enslaved Patriots who joined the British backfired politically.
IV — Major Powers

Who Was Involved?

Opposing
Great Britain
Primary colonial power. British Army and Royal Navy. ~30,000 Hessian mercenaries. 15-20% Loyalist colonists formed militias.
Patriot
The 13 Colonies
Declared independence July 4, 1776. Continental Army under Washington. Initially a civil war before becoming an international conflict.
Allied
France
Most important ally. Treaty of Alliance 1778 after Saratoga. Motivated by revenge for Seven Years War losses. Decisive at Yorktown.
Allied
Spain
Entered 1779 as French ally. Recaptured Baton Rouge, Natchez, Mobile & Pensacola. Opened supply routes through New Orleans.
Allied
Dutch Republic
Dutch merchants supplied loans before official entry (1780). St. Eustatius gave the first international salute to the American flag in 1776.
Opposing
Hessians
~30,000 German soldiers leased to Britain. Roughly 1 in 4 of British forces. Targeted by Washington at Trenton, Christmas 1776.
Mixed
Native Nations
Most sided with Britain. Iroquois split: Mohawk/Seneca to British; Oneida/Tuscarora to Patriots. All suffered devastating losses.
Opposing
Loyalists
15-20% of colonists. Formed Provincial Corps and militias. ~60-80k fled to Canada or Britain after the war ended.
V — Driving Factors

What Caused the Revolution?

Economic Causes
I
End of Salutary Neglect
For 150+ years Britain loosely enforced trade laws, allowing near self-governance. After the Seven Years War, Britain demanded the colonies fund their own defense.
II
Navigation Acts (1651)
Restricted colonial trade exclusively to England — a long-standing grievance that set the stage for later taxation conflicts.
III
Post-War Debt
Britain's massive Seven Years War debt led to taxing the colonies, who felt they had already contributed enough to the war effort.
Political Causes
I
Taxation Without Representation
Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Acts (1767), Tea Act (1773) imposed without colonial seats in Parliament. "No taxation without representation" became the rallying cry.
II
Boston Massacre & Tea Party
1770: British soldiers killed 5 colonists. 1773: Colonists dumped British tea in Boston Harbor. Britain responded with the Intolerable Acts.
III
First Continental Congress
Colonies united as a single body for the first time in 1774. Asserted Britain could not subdue Massachusetts alone. Demanded repeal of the Coercive Acts.
Ideological Causes
I
Enlightenment Ideas
John Locke argued governments derive power from consent of the governed and people have the right to overthrow unjust rulers — deeply influencing colonial leaders.
II
Growing Cultural Divide
A widening split between British and American identities eroded colonial loyalty to the Crown. Americans increasingly saw themselves as a distinct people.
III
Common Sense (1776)
Thomas Paine's unprecedented bestseller directly called for independence. Denounced King George III and swayed countless fence-sitting colonists to the Patriot cause.
VI — Consequences

What Did It Change?

Political Impact
The Birth of a Republic
  • Victory led to the drafting of the U.S. Constitution
  • Popularized concepts of rights, sovereignty, and separation of powers
  • Demonstrated a colony could successfully overthrow an empire
  • Directly inspired the French Revolution and global independence movements
Social Impact
Freedom's Contradictions
  • Profound slavery paradox: northern states began abolishing it while the southern economy became dependent on it, setting stage for the Civil War
  • Removal of British protection opened the frontier to unchecked expansion, resulting in hostile policies against Indigenous peoples
  • Elevated importance of female education to raise informed citizens
Economic Impact
A New Commercial Order
  • Freed the nation from British mercantilism
  • The U.S. forged new international trade routes and developed independent domestic manufacturing
  • The Revolution left the central government and states with extreme debt
  • Caused short-term instability and domestic uprisings including Shays Rebellion
Global Legacy
The World Remade
  • Proved that Enlightenment ideals could be put into governing practice
  • The U.S. Constitution became a model for democratic governance worldwide
  • The French Revolution (1789) was directly inspired by the American example
  • Sparked independence movements across Latin America and beyond
